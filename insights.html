Portfolio

Why diversification works, and when it doesn’t

4 min read · Beginner

Diversification is often called the only free lunch in finance. Combine assets whose prices don’t move in perfect lockstep, and their ups and downs partly cancel out. A portfolio’s expected return is simply the weighted average of its holdings’ expected returns, but its risk is usually lower than the weighted average of their risks.

The math in one line

σp² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2·w₁·w₂·ρ·σ₁·σ₂σ = volatility · w = portfolio weight · ρ = correlation between the two assets (−1 to +1)

The last term is where diversification lives. When the correlation ρ is +1, the assets move together and portfolio volatility equals the weighted average of the two. Any correlation below +1 pulls portfolio volatility below that average. At −1, the risk could in theory cancel out entirely.

What diversification cannot remove

Risk comes in two kinds. Idiosyncratic risk is company-specific, such as a failed product launch or a lawsuit, and it shrinks as you add more independent holdings. Systematic risk comes from recessions, interest-rate shocks and broad sell-offs. These hit almost everything at once and remain however many stocks you own. That is why even a portfolio of 50 stocks can fall sharply in a bear market.

When it fails: correlations aren’t constant

Correlations estimated in calm markets tend to rise during crises, when investors sell risky assets together. In 2008, many asset classes that had looked only loosely related fell at the same time. A sound diversification analysis stress-tests portfolios with higher correlations than the historical average suggests.

Key takeaways

  • Portfolio expected return is a weighted average; portfolio risk usually is not.
  • The lower the correlation, the larger the diversification benefit.
  • Diversification reduces company-specific risk, not market-wide risk.
  • Correlations can jump in a crisis, so stress-test them.

Risk

Value at Risk: one number, three caveats

4 min read · Intermediate

Value at Risk (VaR) compresses a portfolio’s downside into a single figure. A one-day 95% VaR of $2,000 means that, under the model’s assumptions, the portfolio is expected to lose more than $2,000 on about one trading day in twenty. Banks, asset managers and regulators have used VaR since the 1990s because it is easy to communicate and to compare across portfolios.

Three ways to calculate it

  • Historical simulation: replay today’s portfolio over past daily returns (say, the last 500 days) and read off the 5th-percentile loss. It needs no distributional assumption, but it assumes that window is representative.
  • Parametric (variance–covariance): assume normally distributed returns and use volatilities and correlations. It is fast, but it underestimates fat tails.
  • Monte Carlo: simulate thousands of scenarios from a chosen model. It is flexible enough for options and other non-linear positions, but only as good as the model.

A worked example

Take a hypothetical $100,000 portfolio with a daily volatility of 1.2%, and assume normally distributed returns with a mean of zero. The one-tailed 95% z-score is 1.645:

VaR₉₅ = z × σdaily × V = 1.645 × 0.012 × $100,000 ≈ $1,974z = standard-normal quantile · σ = daily volatility · V = portfolio value

The three caveats

  1. It says nothing about how bad the tail is. VaR gives the threshold, not the size of losses beyond it. Expected Shortfall (ES), the average loss in the worst cases, fills that gap. The Basel Committee’s Fundamental Review of the Trading Book uses 97.5% ES for banks’ internal-model market-risk capital.
  2. Normality is optimistic. Real returns have fat tails, so extreme days happen far more often than a normal distribution predicts. Parametric VaR can therefore understate risk.
  3. Scaling over time is an approximation. Turning one-day VaR into ten-day VaR by multiplying by √10 assumes returns are independent and identically distributed. That breaks down when volatility clusters.

Key takeaways

  • VaR is a loss threshold at a chosen confidence level and horizon.
  • Historical, parametric and Monte Carlo methods trade simplicity against realism.
  • Pair VaR with Expected Shortfall and stress tests to see the tail.

Markets

Algorithmic trading: what the algorithm actually does

4 min read · Beginner

An algorithmic trading system is a set of explicit rules, executed by software, that decides what to trade, when and how. Algorithms now account for a large share of trading volume in major equity markets. They fall into two families with very different goals.

Execution algorithms: how to trade

A fund that needs to buy 500,000 shares cannot send one giant order without pushing the price against itself. Execution algorithms split large orders into smaller pieces:

  • TWAP (time-weighted average price) spreads the order evenly across a time window.
  • VWAP (volume-weighted average price) trades in proportion to the market’s typical volume through the day.
  • Implementation shortfall algorithms balance the market impact of trading quickly against the price risk of trading slowly.

Strategy algorithms: what to trade

  • Trend following buys assets that have been rising and sells those that have been falling, betting that momentum persists.
  • Mean reversion bets that prices that moved unusually far from a typical level will move back.
  • Statistical arbitrage trades groups of related securities whose prices have drifted from their historical relationship.
  • Market making continuously quotes buy and sell prices, earning the spread while managing inventory risk.

A simple rule, and why backtests mislead

A classic beginner strategy is the moving-average crossover:

if MA50(price) > MA200(price): hold the asset
else: hold cashMAn = average closing price over the last n trading days

Testing a rule like this on historical data, called backtesting, is essential, but it is easy to fool yourself. The most common traps:

  • Look-ahead bias: using information that wasn’t available at the time, such as trading at the open on that day’s closing price.
  • Survivorship bias: testing only on companies that still exist today, which silently excludes the failures.
  • Overfitting: trying many parameter combinations until one looks great on past data by chance.
  • Ignoring costs: commissions, bid–ask spreads and slippage can erase an apparent edge.

Key takeaways

  • Execution algorithms minimize trading costs; strategy algorithms seek returns.
  • A backtest is a hypothesis test, not proof of future performance.
  • Always model costs, and guard against look-ahead, survivorship and overfitting.

Data

Clean returns first: the groundwork of financial data analysis

4 min read · Beginner

Most of the work in financial analysis happens before any model runs. Prices must be adjusted, converted into returns and checked, because small data errors compound into large analytical ones.

From prices to returns

Analysts work with returns rather than raw prices because returns are comparable across assets and closer to stationary, meaning their statistical properties change less over time. Always start from adjusted prices that account for stock splits and dividends. Otherwise a 2-for-1 split looks like a 50% crash.

Simple return:  Rt = Pt / Pt−1 − 1
Log return:    rt = ln(Pt / Pt−1)Log returns add up across time; simple returns add up across the assets in a portfolio.

Three numbers every analysis should report

  • Annualized volatility: the standard deviation of daily returns × √252, the approximate number of trading days in a year. This assumes returns are independent from day to day.
  • Sharpe ratio: (portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ volatility, the excess return earned per unit of risk taken.
  • Maximum drawdown: the largest peak-to-trough fall in value, which is closest to how losses actually feel to an investor.

A data-hygiene checklist

  • Align trading calendars and time zones before combining markets.
  • Handle missing prices explicitly. Forward-filling stale prices can understate volatility.
  • Investigate outliers before removing them. In finance, extreme days are often real, and they matter most.
  • Include delisted securities when the question requires it, to avoid survivorship bias.

Key takeaways

  • Use adjusted prices and work in returns.
  • Annualize volatility with √252, and state your assumptions.
  • Report risk alongside return: volatility, Sharpe ratio and drawdown together.

Machine learning

Machine learning in finance: signal vs. overfitting

4 min read · Intermediate

Machine learning has transformed some parts of finance and barely touched others. The difference usually comes down to signal-to-noise: ML excels where patterns are stable and data is plentiful, and struggles where they are not.

Where it works well

  • Fraud detection: flagging unusual card transactions in real time, learned from millions of labeled examples.
  • Credit scoring: estimating default probability from borrower and loan characteristics.
  • Natural language processing: extracting sentiment and key facts from earnings calls, filings and news.
  • Volatility forecasting: volatility is more persistent, and therefore more predictable, than returns themselves.

Why predicting returns is hard

Market returns are dominated by noise, relationships shift as economic regimes change, and any profitable pattern attracts traders who compete it away. A model with thousands of parameters can memorize that noise and still look excellent in a backtest.

Validate as if time matters

Standard k-fold cross-validation shuffles the data, letting a model learn from the future to predict the past. Financial models need time-aware validation:

  • Walk-forward testing: train on data up to a date, test on the period after it, then roll forward.
  • Purging and embargo: drop training observations whose labels overlap the test period, plus a buffer after it, to prevent leakage.
  • Honest baselines: compare against simple benchmarks, such as buy-and-hold or yesterday’s value, not against zero.

Explainability is not optional

Financial models face scrutiny from risk managers, auditors and regulators, and in lending many jurisdictions require firms to explain why an application was declined. Interpretable models, or explanation tools such as SHAP values, can matter as much as raw accuracy.

Key takeaways

  • ML adds most value where data is rich and patterns are stable.
  • Never shuffle time-series data when validating. Test forward in time.
  • Interpretability and governance matter as much as accuracy.
